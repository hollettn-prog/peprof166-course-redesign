Welcome to Your Teaching Journey

Learning Materials

What do PE, health, and adapted PE teachers help students learn, and how can we recognize that learning?

You bring your own experiences with physical education and health to this course. You may already know which teaching area interests you, or you may still be exploring. Start by looking at a few examples of the work.

For now, your job is to notice and recognize. You will have opportunities later in your program to plan and teach complete lessons. Here, you will see examples and practice making small choices with support.

Learning focus: Recognize what students are learning in examples of PE, health, and adapted PE teaching.

1. Meet three teaching areas

The following examples are fictional. They show a small part of each area; teachers’ work includes many other topics and responsibilities.

Physical education: learning through movement

In an elementary PE lesson, students practice sending a ball toward a target. The teacher demonstrates, gives them time to try, and offers one tip at a time. Students try again and notice where the ball goes.

Notice: The activity is target practice. The learning is improving how students send the ball toward the target. PE can help students develop movement skills, movement knowledge, and ways to participate with others.

Health education: practicing skills for everyday situations

In a middle school health lesson, students read a fictional situation in which a friend pressures someone to do something they do not want to do. The teacher models a clear, respectful response. Partners then practice using a provided response starter.

Notice: Students are practicing communication. They are doing more than recalling a definition. Health education includes knowledge and skills students can use in everyday situations. Students can practice with fictional situations without sharing private experiences.

Adapted physical education: supporting access to PE learning

In a fictional PE lesson, a student is working on sending a ball toward a target. An adapted PE teacher helps identify a ball and target distance that let the student practice successfully. The student practices, and the teacher observes what happens.

Notice: The learning focus is still sending a ball toward a target. The teacher adjusts the practice conditions to support that learner. Adapted PE focuses on PE instruction that addresses individual needs of learners with disabilities. This example introduces the work; it does not ask you to determine services or design an individual program.

Connection: These areas can overlap. For these examples, look at the main learning focus rather than only the room, equipment, or teacher’s title.

2. Work through an example with us

Situation: A teacher says, “Today, you will practice sending a ball toward a target.” After a demonstration, students take turns practicing. The teacher watches and gives a brief tip before the next attempt.

Question: Which statement tells us what students are learning?

  1. Students take turns in pairs.
  2. Students improve how they send a ball toward a target.
  3. Students use a ball and a floor marker.
  4. Students practice until the teacher signals to stop.

Walk-through: First, find the action students are practicing: sending the ball. Next, find what they are trying to improve: sending it toward a target. That makes B the best answer.

A tells us how students are organized. C names equipment. D describes a routine. All can be part of the lesson, but B identifies the learning.

A useful question: “What are students getting better at?” Ask this when you see a lesson example. You do not need to write your own learning objective yet.

3. Notice what the teacher does

Across the three examples, the teacher helps students learn by:

  • Showing: giving a demonstration or a sample response.
  • Providing practice: letting students try the skill.
  • Giving feedback: offering a useful tip for the next attempt.
  • Supporting participation: adjusting materials or directions when needed.

Teachers also prepare materials, organize spaces and transitions, communicate with others, and complete responsibilities beyond teaching a lesson. We will explore those roles further as we learn about the profession.

Takeaway

PE, health, and adapted PE teachers help students learn and practice. Activities, equipment, and routines support that learning. As you explore the profession, keep asking: “What are students learning, and what is the teacher doing to help?”
