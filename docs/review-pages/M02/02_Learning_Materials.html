What Does a Teacher Actually Do?

When you were in school, you probably saw your teachers leading lessons, answering questions, and managing students. But much of their work happened before or after you were in the classroom.

Teachers plan lessons, assess student progress, communicate with families, work with other professionals, and make sure students have opportunities to learn.

Let's look at some of those responsibilities.

Big Question

What responsibilities do teachers have that students might never see?

A Teacher's Work Goes Beyond the Lesson

  • Planning: Deciding what students should learn and choosing activities to help them learn it.
  • Teaching: Explaining, demonstrating, asking questions, and helping students practice.
  • Assessment: Checking what students understand or can do, then deciding what they need next.
  • Communication: Working respectfully with students, families, colleagues, and school staff.
  • Student support: Helping students access learning and following required safety and support procedures.
  • Professional growth: Continuing to learn new teaching approaches and improve instruction.

Example: More Than Teaching a PE Lesson

Mr. Davis teaches elementary physical education. Today, his students are practicing throwing and catching.

Before Class

He prepares equipment, reviews his learning goal, and checks whether any students need adjustments to the activity.

During Class

He demonstrates throwing, gives feedback, helps students who are struggling, and watches for safety concerns.

After Class

He reviews what students could do and considers what to teach next. He also communicates with another teacher about a student's progress.

Think about it: Which of these responsibilities would you have noticed when you were a student?

How Do You Become a Teacher in Wisconsin?

Wisconsin public school teachers must hold the appropriate educator license. The Wisconsin Department of Public Instruction (DPI) oversees educator licensing.

There are several steps between starting college and becoming a licensed teacher.

Your General Pathway

  1. Choose a teaching program.
    Learn which subjects and grade levels your program prepares you to teach.
  2. Complete your coursework.
    Study teaching, learning, PE, health, adapted PE, and other required subjects.
  3. Gain classroom experience.
    Observe teachers, practice teaching, and complete required field experiences and student teaching.
  4. Meet licensing requirements.
    Complete the requirements that apply to your program and the license you are seeking.
  5. Apply for your license.
    After completing an approved preparation pathway and receiving the required endorsement, apply through Wisconsin DPI.

Important: This is a general overview. Specific licensing requirements can change and may differ by teaching area or preparation pathway. Use Wisconsin DPI and your university's advising and licensure office for current requirements.

What About PE, Health, and Adapted PE?

These teaching areas are connected, but they are not identical. Teachers need preparation that matches the subjects and students they will teach.

Teaching Area What Teachers Need to Understand
Physical Education Movement skills, physical activity, fitness, learning progressions, and safe instruction.
Health Education Health information, health skills, age-appropriate instruction, and responsible classroom practices.
Adapted Physical Education Specially designed PE instruction, individual learning needs, disability-related supports, and collaboration with other professionals.

UW-Whitewater's HPEA program prepares students for professional work across these areas. Your advisor can help you understand the specific program and licensing requirements.

The Profession Has Changed Over Time

Physical education has not always looked the way it does today. Programs have emphasized different goals over time, including physical training, sports, fitness, and the development of movement skills for lifelong activity.

Expectations about who should participate and how schools support students with disabilities have also changed. Laws, professional standards, and research continue to influence how teachers work.

As you move through the program, you'll learn more about these changes and the responsibilities they create for teachers.

Why Do Teachers Advocate for Their Programs?

Sometimes teachers need to explain why their subject deserves time, equipment, funding, or support.

For example, imagine a school is considering reducing PE time to make room for another subject.

A PE teacher might explain how regular, well-planned instruction helps students develop movement skills, confidence, and the knowledge needed to participate in physical activity throughout life.

This is an example of advocacy: explaining the value of a program and supporting opportunities for students.

Teaching Takeaway

Teachers do much more than lead activities. They plan, communicate, support students, make professional decisions, and continue learning. Becoming a teacher takes preparation, practice, and a willingness to improve.
